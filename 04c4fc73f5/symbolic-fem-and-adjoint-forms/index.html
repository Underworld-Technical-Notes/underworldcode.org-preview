Symbolic FEM and Adjoint Forms

Australian National University
October 25, 2026
https://doi.org/10.6084/m9.figshare.34785585
Photo by Logan Voss / Unsplash

In this article, we will see how the underworld3 sympy formulation combined with the PETSc finite element, strong-form framework builds model derivatives which can solve non-linear problems using a Newton method. This requires us to calculate the local derivative of the model with respect to the unknowns so that we know the direction to move towards a better solution.

If we differentiate the prediction a model makes with respect to one of its parameters, then we can determine how sensitive the prediction is to changes in that parameter and therefore whether the model can be used to constrain the value of that parameter. Similarly, model derivatives are used in data assimilation to determine how to adjust a model’s parameters so it better fits the observations we have 12.

The forward problem, solved by Newton¶

Underworld3 starts from the strong form of the equations which is usually how they are written in a textbook or a paper. These are symbolic, sympy objects that pass directly to compiled objects in PETSc 34: everything inside a divergence term goes into the flux F1\mathbf F_1F1​, everything else into the force F0F_0F0​. The discrete solution is written in the finite-element basis, u=∑jujϕju = \sum_j u_j \phi_ju=∑j​uj​ϕj​, and the weak form is one equation for each basis function ϕi\phi_iϕi​: the integral of the two templates against it and its gradient,

Ri(u; m)  =  ∫ΩF0(u,∇u; m) ϕi  +  F1(u,∇u; m)⋅∇ϕi  =  0.R_i(u;\, m) \;=\; \int_\Omega F_0(u, \nabla u;\, m)\,\phi_i \;+\; \mathbf F_1(u, \nabla u;\, m) \cdot \nabla\phi_i \;=\; 0 .Ri​(u;m)=∫Ω​F0​(u,∇u;m)ϕi​+F1​(u,∇u;m)⋅∇ϕi​=0.
(1)

RRR, the residual (1), is the out-of-balance force: what the assembled system has left over when the current uuu is put into the governing equation. A solution is a uuu that makes it vanish. Here mmm is a set of parameters of the model (for example a Rayleigh number, or a constitutive modulus) that the user typically would adjust to run an example.

A Newton non-linear solver needs the derivative of RRR with respect to the unknowns 5:

G0=∂F0∂u,G1=∂F0∂(∇u),G2=∂F1∂u,G3=∂F1∂(∇u),G_0 = \frac{\partial F_0}{\partial u}, \quad G_1 = \frac{\partial F_0}{\partial (\nabla u)}, \quad G_2 = \frac{\partial \mathbf F_1}{\partial u}, \quad G_3 = \frac{\partial \mathbf F_1}{\partial (\nabla u)} ,G0​=∂u∂F0​​,G1​=∂(∇u)∂F0​​,G2​=∂u∂F1​​,G3​=∂(∇u)∂F1​​,
(2)

which underworld computes directly from sympy, compiles and registers with PETSc. The Jacobian is the derivative of each equation RiR_iRi​ with respect to each nodal unknown uju_juj​. Since ∂u/∂uj=ϕj\partial u/\partial u_j = \phi_j∂u/∂uj​=ϕj​ and ∂(∇u)/∂uj=∇ϕj\partial (\nabla u)/\partial u_j = \nabla\phi_j∂(∇u)/∂uj​=∇ϕj​, the chain rule turns the four blocks into Kij=∂Ri/∂ujK_{ij} = \partial R_i/\partial u_jKij​=∂Ri​/∂uj​, assembled element by element,

Kij=∫Ωϕi G0 ϕj  +  ϕi G1⋅∇ϕj  +  ∇ϕi⋅G2 ϕj  +  ∇ϕi⋅G3⋅∇ϕj.K_{ij} = \int_\Omega \phi_i \, G_0 \, \phi_j \;+\; \phi_i \, G_1 \cdot \nabla\phi_j \;+\; \nabla\phi_i \cdot G_2 \, \phi_j \;+\; \nabla\phi_i \cdot G_3 \cdot \nabla\phi_j .Kij​=∫Ω​ϕi​G0​ϕj​+ϕi​G1​⋅∇ϕj​+∇ϕi​⋅G2​ϕj​+∇ϕi​⋅G3​⋅∇ϕj​.
(3)

K=∂R/∂uK = \partial R/\partial uK=∂R/∂u, assembled by (3), is the tangent stiffness matrix. For a linear problem it is just the stiffness matrix, and

R=Ku−fR = Ku - fR=Ku−f
(4)

For a nonlinear problem, K(u)K(u)K(u) is the stiffness of the material at the current solution state. Newton uses it to reduce the out-of-balance force: expanding R(u+δu)≈R(u)+K δuR(u + \delta u) \approx R(u) + K\,\delta uR(u+δu)≈R(u)+Kδu and asking for the left side to vanish gives the correction

K δu=−R(u),u←u+δu,K\,\delta u = -R(u), \qquad u \leftarrow u + \delta u ,Kδu=−R(u),u←u+δu,
(5)

repeated until RRR is zero to the tolerance asked for. For a linear problem (4) makes the expansion exact, and one step gives the solution.

The adjoint problem¶

Now suppose we want to choose the parameters mmm so that the model reproduces some observations as well as it can. The observations are values diobsd_i^{\mathrm{obs}}diobs​ — a surface velocity at a station, or a stress at a point — and the model predicts the same quantities, di(u)d_i(u)di​(u), from its solution. The quality of the fit is the misfit

J(u)=12∑i(di(u)−diobs)2,J(u) = \frac12 \sum_i \big(d_i(u) - d_i^{\mathrm{obs}}\big)^2 ,J(u)=21​i∑​(di​(u)−diobs​)2,
(6)

and the task is to minimise this by adjusting mmm, under the constraint that uuu always solves the model: R(u; m)=0R(u;\, m) = 0R(u;m)=0 at every mmm we try. To do that we need the gradient dJ/dm\mathrm dJ/\mathrm dmdJ/dm (which way, and how far, to move each parameter) and the adjoint approach provides this for all the parameters at once - at the expense of one additional linear solve.

The constraint is added to the minimisation with a Lagrange multiplier μ\muμ 6:

L(u,μ; m)=J(u; m)+μTR(u; m),\mathcal L(u, \mu;\, m) = J(u;\, m) + \mu^{T} R(u;\, m) ,L(u,μ;m)=J(u;m)+μTR(u;m),
(7)

which equals JJJ for any μ\muμ whenever the model is solved, because R=0R = 0R=0 there. We are free to choose μ\muμ, and we choose it so that L\mathcal LL does not change, to first order, when the solution changes: ∂L/∂u=∂J/∂u+μTK=0\partial\mathcal L/\partial u = \partial J/\partial u + \mu^{T}K = 0∂L/∂u=∂J/∂u+μTK=0. Transposed, that is the adjoint problem,

KTμ=−(∂J∂u)T,K^{T}\mu = -\Big(\frac{\partial J}{\partial u}\Big)^{T},KTμ=−(∂u∂J​)T,
(8)

a linear solve for a field μ\muμ on the same mesh as uuu. It is linear because uuu is already known: KKK and ∂J/∂u\partial J/\partial u∂J/∂u are evaluated at the converged solution and held fixed, and μ\muμ appears only multiplied by them. The operator is the tangent stiffness KKK at that solution, transposed. The force is the misfit’s derivative with respect to the solution,

∂J∂uj=∑i(di(u)−diobs) ∂di∂uj,\frac{\partial J}{\partial u_j} = \sum_i \big(d_i(u) - d_i^{\mathrm{obs}}\big)\,\frac{\partial d_i}{\partial u_j} ,∂uj​∂J​=i∑​(di​(u)−diobs​)∂uj​∂di​​,
(9)

one entry per nodal unknown: each misfit di−diobsd_i - d_i^{\mathrm{obs}}di​−diobs​, placed at the nodes its prediction depends on. This is the misfit written as a force, assembled like any finite-element force vector, and it is the only place the observations enter. With μ\muμ chosen this way, the solution’s response to mmm drops out of dL/dm\mathrm d\mathcal L/\mathrm dmdL/dm, and what is left is the gradient for each parameter,

dJdmk=∂J∂mk+μT∂R∂mk,\frac{\mathrm d J}{\mathrm d m_k} = \frac{\partial J}{\partial m_k} + \mu^{T}\frac{\partial R}{\partial m_k} ,dmk​dJ​=∂mk​∂J​+μT∂mk​∂R​,
(10)

where ∂R/∂mk\partial R/\partial m_k∂R/∂mk​ is a pseudo-force: the out-of-balance force that appears when mkm_kmk​ is nudged and the solution held still. It is an integral for each parameter, not a solve.

The appendix reaches (8) and (10) a second way, by differentiating the constraint directly, which shows what each term means.

Reducing JJJ is then a loop, Figure 2: (1) solve the model, (2) solve the adjoint problem, (3) form the gradient for every parameter, (4) update the parameters and go round again. In practice a quasi-Newton method such as L-BFGS chooses the step α\alphaα and builds up curvature from successive gradients.

Each pass costs one nonlinear solve and one linear one, irrespective of the number of parameters there are, and the fit improves until the gradient vanishes, at a minimum of JJJ where no small change of mmm brings the model closer to the observations. The minimum may be a local one: the gradient describes the surface only where the model is. In Underworld3, with the misfit J written as an integrand over the mesh, steps 2 and 3 are a single call,

dJdm = stokes.gradient(J, parameters=[m1, m2, m3])["parameters"]
dJdm[m1]                       # dJ/dm1

and step 4 is whatever optimiser one prefers.

Re-using the forward solve¶

The adjoint problem (8) asks for two things: the transposed stiffness KTK^{T}KT, and the pseudo-force ∂R/∂m\partial R/\partial m∂R/∂m. Neither is a new object. Both are the blocks (2) that Newton already uses, read differently.

The transposed stiffness is a relabelling. KTK^{T}KT is the bilinear form of (3) with trial and test exchanged. Exchanging ϕi\phi_iϕi​ and ϕj\phi_jϕj​ leaves the two diagonal terms in place, transposed on their paired indices, and swaps the two mixed ones — the G1G_1G1​ term becomes a G2G_2G2​ term and the other way about, with their components exchanged:

G0  →  G0T,G3  →  G3T,G1  →  G2T,G2  →  G1T.G_0 \;\rightarrow\; G_0^{T}, \qquad G_3 \;\rightarrow\; G_3^{T}, \qquad G_1 \;\rightarrow\; G_2^{T}, \qquad G_2 \;\rightarrow\; G_1^{T} .G0​→G0T​,G3​→G3T​,G1​→G2T​,G2​→G1T​.
(11)

So the adjoint operator is assembled from the same four compiled kernels, registered in a different arrangement. We do that wherever the kernels carry the whole operator, rather than transposing an assembled matrix, which matters for Stokes, where the (u,p)(u,p)(u,p) and (p,u)(p,u)(p,u) blocks exchange places by the same rule and the composite operator stays a composite operator the Krylov solver can be preconditioned against. Nothing is differentiated a second time, and the assembled operator agrees with the explicit transpose to machine precision.

The parameter derivative is the same diff, on a different symbol. A named quantity mmm — a friction coefficient, a reference viscosity, a continuation parameter — survives into F0F_0F0​ and F1\mathbf F_1F1​ as a symbol, because the solver carries the constitutive model’s own expression rather than the number it currently holds. Differentiating (1) with respect to mmm instead of uuu is the same operation that produced (2):

∂R∂m  =  ∫Ω∂F0∂m ϕ  +  ∂F1∂m⋅∇ϕ.\frac{\partial R}{\partial m} \;=\; \int_\Omega \frac{\partial F_0}{\partial m}\,\phi \;+\; \frac{\partial \mathbf F_1}{\partial m}\cdot\nabla\phi .∂m∂R​=∫Ω​∂m∂F0​​ϕ+∂m∂F1​​⋅∇ϕ.
(12)

Accuracy of the gradient¶

Three things decide the accuracy of the gradient. The forward solve must be converged before the adjoint is taken, because μ\muμ is the multiplier for the constraint R=0R = 0R=0 and has meaning only where that holds. The adjoint solve itself can be run to a loose tolerance: every pass re-solves the forward problem and recomputes JJJ, so a direction that is slightly off costs an extra iteration and the next misfit shows whether it helped. And the matrix must be the true derivative ∂R/∂u\partial R/\partial u∂R/∂u. With a frozen-viscosity Picard matrix, μ\muμ solves its own equation exactly and the gradient it gives belongs to no function, so the optimiser can stop where the true gradient is still large. The tangent is a property of the code rather than of the iterate, so it is checked once, by finite differences, and assembling it symbolically from the same residual is what makes it right by construction.

Derivation of the adjoint¶

The adjoint problem (8) and the gradient (10) also follow from differentiating the constraint directly, without the Lagrangian (7), and this route shows what each term means.

We explicitly wrote RRR in terms of mmm to demonstrate that there is an entire family of solutions to R=0R=0R=0 that we can access by changing the model’s free parameters. If the model makes a prediction, J(u)J(u)J(u), we can ask how changing mmm affects that prediction (the sensitivity of the prediction to mmm). That is

dJdm\frac{\mathrm d J}{\mathrm d m}dmdJ​
(13)

If JJJ is an underworld function then we can form a symbolic expression. First we note that uuu and mmm are not independent: the solution is constrained to the family of solutions of (1), R=0R = 0R=0 holds for every mmm and, because of this, dR/dmdR/dmdR/dm is zero too

dRdm=∂R∂ududm+∂R∂m=0.\frac{\mathrm d R}{\mathrm d m} = \frac{\partial R}{\partial u}\frac{\mathrm d u}{\mathrm d m} + \frac{\partial R}{\partial m} = 0 .dmdR​=∂u∂R​dmdu​+∂m∂R​=0.
(14)

The first factor is the tangent stiffness KKK again, so

K dudm=−∂R∂m,sodudm=−K−1∂R∂m,K\,\frac{\mathrm d u}{\mathrm d m} = -\frac{\partial R}{\partial m}, \qquad \text{so} \qquad \frac{\mathrm d u}{\mathrm d m} = -K^{-1}\frac{\partial R}{\partial m},Kdmdu​=−∂m∂R​,sodmdu​=−K−1∂m∂R​,
(15)

∂R/∂m\partial R/\partial m∂R/∂m is the pseudo-force of (10): the out-of-balance force that appears if the parameter is nudged and the solution is held still. It is assembled exactly as RRR is, with ∂F0/∂m\partial F_0/\partial m∂F0​/∂m and ∂F1/∂m\partial \mathbf F_1/\partial m∂F1​/∂m in place of F0F_0F0​ and F1\mathbf F_1F1​.

We interpret (15) as: the sensitivity of the solution to a parameter is the response of the structure to that parameter’s pseudo-force.

The prediction depends on mmm in the same two ways: directly, and through the solution. With ∂J/∂u\partial J/\partial u∂J/∂u the row of derivatives ∂J/∂uj\partial J/\partial u_j∂J/∂uj​ with respect to the nodal unknowns — the prediction written as a force — the chain rule and (15) give

dJdm=∂J∂m+∂J∂ududm=∂J∂m−∂J∂u K−1∂R∂m.\frac{\mathrm d J}{\mathrm d m} = \frac{\partial J}{\partial m} + \frac{\partial J}{\partial u}\frac{\mathrm d u}{\mathrm d m} = \frac{\partial J}{\partial m} - \frac{\partial J}{\partial u}\,K^{-1}\frac{\partial R}{\partial m} .dmdJ​=∂m∂J​+∂u∂J​dmdu​=∂m∂J​−∂u∂J​K−1∂m∂R​.
(16)

Many parameters at once¶

A real model has many parameters, m1,…,mNm_1, \dots, m_Nm1​,…,mN​, and we want the sensitivity for all of them. We first write (16) like this:

dJdmk=∂J∂mk−∂J∂u K−1 ∂R∂mk,k=1,…,N.\frac{\mathrm d J}{\mathrm d m_k} = \frac{\partial J}{\partial m_k} - \frac{\partial J}{\partial u}\,K^{-1}\,\frac{\partial R}{\partial m_k}, \qquad k = 1, \dots, N .dmk​dJ​=∂mk​∂J​−∂u∂J​K−1∂mk​∂R​,k=1,…,N.
(17)

These are NNN equations that differ only in their last factor, the pseudo-force ∂R/∂mk\partial R/\partial m_k∂R/∂mk​. The row in front of it, (∂J/∂u)K−1\big(\partial J/\partial u\big)K^{-1}(∂J/∂u)K−1, contains the prediction and the stiffness but no parameter; it is the same for every kkk and we only need to compute it once.

Now define μ\muμ through

μT=−∂J∂u K−1.\mu^{T} = -\frac{\partial J}{\partial u}\,K^{-1} .μT=−∂u∂J​K−1.
(18)

We do not have access to K−1K^{-1}K−1 so we multiply both sides by KKK on the right, take the transpose and substitute into (17) to recover (8):

KTμ=−(∂J∂u)T,dJdmk=∂J∂mk+μT∂R∂mk.K^{T}\mu = -\Big(\frac{\partial J}{\partial u}\Big)^{T}, \qquad \frac{\mathrm d J}{\mathrm d m_k} = \frac{\partial J}{\partial m_k} + \mu^{T}\frac{\partial R}{\partial m_k} .KTμ=−(∂u∂J​)T,dmk​dJ​=∂mk​∂J​+μT∂mk​∂R​.
(19)

One linear solve for μ\muμ, whatever NNN is, and then one product for each parameter. Written as fields, with μ=∑iμiϕi\mu = \sum_i \mu_i \phi_iμ=∑i​μi​ϕi​, that product is

μT∂R∂mk=∫Ω∂F0∂mk μ  +  ∂F1∂mk⋅∇μ,\mu^{T}\frac{\partial R}{\partial m_k} = \int_\Omega \frac{\partial F_0}{\partial m_k}\,\mu \;+\; \frac{\partial \mathbf F_1}{\partial m_k}\cdot\nabla\mu ,μT∂mk​∂R​=∫Ω​∂mk​∂F0​​μ+∂mk​∂F1​​⋅∇μ,
(20)

which is the pseudo-force’s weak form with μ\muμ in the place of the test function.

μ\muμ, the adjoint state, is the response of the same structure to the misfit’s force: an influence field, in the sense of an influence line. It says how strongly an out-of-balance force applied anywhere in the domain would tell on the misfit. With it in hand, the derivative for a parameter is μT∂R/∂m\mu^{T}\partial R/\partial mμT∂R/∂m — the virtual work that parameter’s pseudo-force does against the influence field — and that is an integral, not a solve.

Solving (15) for each du/dmk\mathrm d u/\mathrm d m_kdu/dmk​ and contracting it with ∂J/∂u\partial J/\partial u∂J/∂u gives the same numbers, at the cost of NNN solves. That the two agree is Maxwell–Betti reciprocity: the work done by one force through the displacements of another equals the work done by the second through the displacements of the first. The transpose is what makes it true for a problem whose stiffness is not symmetric.

So the adjoint is cheap in parameters and dear in misfits. One solve for ten parameters or a thousand; another solve for every additional misfit. That suits a model with many dials and one thing you care about, which is the usual shape of the problem.

One stiffness, three forces¶

The Newton update, the forward sensitivity and the adjoint are three linear solves with the same matrix and different right-hand sides:

K δu=−R,K dudm=−∂R∂m,KTμ=−(∂J∂u)T.K\,\delta u = -R, \qquad\quad K\,\frac{\mathrm d u}{\mathrm d m} = -\frac{\partial R}{\partial m}, \qquad\quad K^{T}\mu = -\Big(\frac{\partial J}{\partial u}\Big)^{T} .Kδu=−R,Kdmdu​=−∂m∂R​,KTμ=−(∂u∂J​)T.
(21)

Newton has already assembled and preconditioned that matrix, so the adjoint costs one more linear solve.
